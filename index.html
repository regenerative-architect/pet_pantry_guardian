Five-rule quick guide

Use this when you need the short version before opening the technical explanations.

1. Ingredient beats food name“Peanut butter,” “gum,” “sauce,” and “protein bar” are not safety determinations. Check xylitol, onion, garlic, caffeine, alcohol and chocolate.
2. Pet species changes everythingAvocado is a much larger concern for pet birds/rabbits than dogs; grapes are a major dog hazard; cats are especially susceptible to alliums.
3. Preparation changes riskCooked bones splinter, raw animal foods carry pathogens, dough expands/ferments, and powders can concentrate plant toxins.
4. “Not toxic” ≠ “good food”Fat, salt, sugar and starch can still cause GI upset, obesity or dietary imbalance—especially in small mammals and ferrets.
5. Uncertain means don’t experimentIf the exact product or species is not covered, hold the food and check with a veterinarian familiar with that animal rather than using another species as a proxy.

How to interpret the labels

This is deliberately conservative and does not replace case-specific veterinary toxicology.

Each food card is now generated through two layers: an item/hazard layer and a species-physiology layer. That lets the same food explain a kidney mechanism in one animal, red-cell injury in another, or primarily dietary/GI mismatch in a third. 🚨 Emergency toxic means a well-recognized poisoning pattern or potentially severe hazard where delay can matter. ⛔ Avoid / high risk covers known toxicants, serious mechanical/infectious hazards, or strongly inappropriate foods. ⚠ Caution means not a routine food or a meaningful risk exists depending on amount/preparation. ✓ Generally appropriate means plain, properly prepared, small portions can fit that species’ diet—but not that the item is nutritionally complete or safe in unlimited quantity. ? Evidence/species dependent means the literature is limited, contradictory, or the product is too formulation-dependent for a blanket classification.

No dose calculator by design. Toxicity can depend on body weight, concentration, formulation, individual susceptibility and time since exposure. Grapes/raisins in dogs are a good example of unpredictable sensitivity. For an actual ingestion, a veterinary professional should perform the case-specific calculation.

Evidence register & limitations

Primary anchors are veterinary toxicology manuals, ASPCA/FDA poison guidance and species-specific veterinary diet guidance. Links open online; the atlas itself remains usable offline.

    Scope: common household foods and companion species. This is not an exhaustive toxicology database, and “pet bird” is necessarily broad—avian species differ substantially. Any new or unusual food should be checked with a veterinarian familiar with that species.
